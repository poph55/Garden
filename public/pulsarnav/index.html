MODEL / CALCULATIONS

How three pulsars produce a 2D fix

Each pulsar is treated as a distant clock. Its pulse arrival phase constrains one projection of the spacecraft position; three differently angled projections are combined into the best-fitting location.
01

Convert pulse timing into a projected range

For pulsar i, the direction vector points toward that pulsar. The measured range along that direction is the true position projection plus timing error.

MEASUREMENT MODEL
zi=xcos(θi)+ysin(θi)+εiλi=cPiΔφi=ziλi
zi measured projected rangeθi pulsar direction angleεi timing error

The model assumes a coarse prior has already resolved which whole pulse cycle was observed.

02

Find the point that best fits all three measurements

Each projected range defines a line of possible positions. Noise prevents the three lines from meeting perfectly, so weighted least squares finds the point with the smallest weighted disagreement.

WEIGHTED LEAST-SQUARES SOLUTION
p^=(HTWH)−1HTWz
H three pulsar direction rowsW inverse noise-variance weightsz measured projected ranges
03

Estimate phase from detected photons

The sensor generates pulsed and unpulsed counts in fine phase bins. The unpulsed term combines steady source emission, surrounding sky or nebular emission, and detector background. A Poisson maximum-likelihood search shifts the known pulse template until the pulsed modulation plus that baseline best explains the total detected profile.

Rates use the published SEXTANT NICER-like detector catalog. This milestone bins counts for browser efficiency; individual photon timestamps, energy response, and dead time are the next sensor-processing layer.

04

Turn phase uncertainty into the 95% ellipse

The curvature of each pulsar's phase likelihood sets its projected-range weight. The inverse weighted geometry matrix is the position covariance; its eigenvectors and eigenvalues set the ellipse orientation and axes.
